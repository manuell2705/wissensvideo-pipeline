**2. Gehirn gegen die besten Supercomputer ist jetzt drin**

Laut der aktuellen TOP500-Liste (November 2026) ist **LineShine** in Shenzhen auf Platz 1. Er schafft 2,2 Exaflops und braucht 42 MW. Auf Platz 2 ist El Capitan mit 1,8 Exaflops und 30 MW.

NIST schätzt das Gehirn grob auf "das Äquivalent eines Exaflops" bei 20 Watt. NIST sagt selbst, dass das nur eine grobe Schätzung ist, und so sagen wir es auch im Video. Der Stromunterschied liegt bei etwa dem 2-Millionen-Fachen.

**3. Rhetorik, aber unauffällig**

Ich habe die Tricks aus deinen Referenzen eingebaut. Kein "you won't believe" und keine leeren Versprechen.

- **Kalter Einstieg mit "du":** Das Video startet bei etwas, das gerade in dir passiert, nicht mit einer Behauptung.
- **Roter Faden, die Glühbirne:** Gleich am Anfang heißt es "about as much power as a dim light bulb". Die Glühbirne kommt im Kampf wieder und im allerletzten Satz noch einmal. So fühlt sich das Video rund an.
- **Offene Schleife:** Am Anfang wird der Supercomputer nur kurz angedeutet. Aufgelöst wird das erst am Ende.
- **Greifbare Zahlen:** "a fire hose feeding a drinking straw" und "roughly two million times less power".
- **Kontrast und Wendung:** Erst die Erwartung, dann die Umkehr, zum Beispiel "Not genius. Chaos." oder "Forgetting isn't a bug."
- **Rhythmus:** Auf einen langen Satz folgt ein kurzer Schlag. Dazu kommt alle 30 bis 40 Sekunden ein kleiner Humor-Moment.
- **Mini-Cliffhanger:** Am Ende jedes Kapitels steht eine Brücke zum nächsten. Das "Level one, Level two" fällt weg, weil es zu sehr nach Spiel und Liste klingt.
- **Ehrliche Einschränkungen:** Zum Beispiel "Nobody can measure this exactly." Das wirkt glaubwürdig statt reißerisch.

## Das Skript (Englisch, für George)

### Hook
1. Right now, a few centimetres behind your eyes, about eighty-six billion cells are deciding what you'll notice next.
2. They run on roughly twenty watts. About as much power as a dim light bulb.
3. And yet, you've probably heard that you only use ten percent of them.
4. So what if you could switch on the rest? Sharper senses. Perfect memory. Maybe even enough to keep up with the fastest computer on Earth.
5. To find out, we first have to ask a stranger question: what is the other ninety percent actually doing?

### Title (Sting)

### The myth
6. The ten percent idea is everywhere. Films, ads, motivational videos.
7. It probably started with early psychologists, who wrote that people use only a small part of their mental potential. Somewhere along the way, "potential" quietly turned into a number.
8. Here's the problem: brain scans show that over a single day, practically every region gets busy. There is no silent ninety percent.
9. But not all at once. At any given moment, only a small share of your neurons is firing. And that's not laziness. It's a budget.
10. Your brain is about two percent of your body weight, yet it uses around twenty percent of your energy. Switching everything on at once would cost more than your body could ever pay.
11. So let's cheat. Let's pretend the budget doesn't exist, and look at what would happen, one part at a time. We'll keep the science honest. Only the rules are fantasy.

### 1. Senses
12. Start with what's coming in.
13. Your eyes, ears and skin feed your brain an estimated billion bits of information every second.
14. Your conscious mind works with about ten. It's like a fire hose feeding a drinking straw.
15. So imagine the straw disappears. Every sound in the room, every flicker of light, the feeling of your socks. All at once, all the time.
16. It wouldn't feel like a superpower. It would feel like standing inside a fire alarm. That filter is the reason you can hear your own name across a noisy room.
17. Some people do experience a small crossover. With synaesthesia, sounds can have colours, and numbers can have a place in space. Interesting, but not super-speed.
18. So more input isn't the upgrade. Better filtering is. Which raises the next question: what about everything you do let in?

### 2. Memory
19. What if you never forgot anything?
20. A few people come close. With a rare condition called hyperthymesia, they can recall almost every day of their adult lives.
21. Name a random Tuesday from years ago, and they can tell you what they did. Sometimes even what the weather was like.
22. Only a few dozen cases have been confirmed by scientists. And many of them describe it as a mixed gift. Painful memories don't fade either.
23. In the nineteen twenties, a Russian journalist named Solomon Shereshevsky could repeat long lists of numbers, even years later. But he struggled to forget. Sometimes even a normal conversation was hard to follow, because every word set off a flood of images.
24. And here's the twist. Your memory isn't a hard drive. Every time you remember something, your brain rebuilds it, a little like retelling a story.
25. Forgetting isn't a bug. It's your brain tidying up, so the important things stand out. Which is lucky, because there's something it needs that space for: speed.

### 3. Speed
26. Your fastest nerve signals travel at up to one hundred and twenty metres per second. That's faster than a Formula One car at top speed.
27. And still, it takes you about a quarter of a second to react. See the ball, move your hand.
28. Even "now" arrives a little late. Your brain takes a fraction of a second to assemble each moment, then quietly edits it so sound and sight feel perfectly in sync.
29. So let's upgrade. Neurons that fire ten times faster. Sounds great, until you remember that more firing means more energy. And more energy means more heat.
30. Your brain works best in a narrow temperature range. A few degrees too warm, and thinking gets confused. Further than that, and it becomes dangerous.
31. Your brain found a smarter answer long ago. It doesn't work faster. It works wider. Eighty-six billion neurons, around a hundred trillion connections, all working in parallel.

### 4. Body
32. Your brain isn't only a thinker. It's also a bodyguard.
33. Scientists think it normally holds back how hard your muscles can pull, because full force could damage tendons and bones.
34. And it quietly runs a hundred things you never notice. Heartbeat. Breathing. Digestion. If you had to manage all of that yourself, there wouldn't be much brain left for anything else.
35. In extreme moments, with adrenaline flooding in, people have reported sudden bursts of strength. Scientists think the brain may loosen that limit for a moment. Mostly, the evidence is stories, not lab data.
36. Now imagine no limit at all. And no pain either. Sounds powerful, until you remember what pain is for.
37. People born unable to feel pain often get badly hurt without noticing. Pain isn't the enemy. It's the alarm.

### 5. Learning
38. But there is one upgrade you already have. And it's the one that matters most.
39. Every time you practise something, your brain physically rewires itself.
40. London taxi drivers, who memorise thousands of streets, were found to have a larger rear part of the hippocampus, a region involved in navigation.
41. You don't need a taxi licence. In one study, people who learned to juggle for three months showed measurable changes in their brain scans.
42. And while you sleep, your brain replays the day and keeps what matters. A rested brain isn't at a hundred percent. But it's a lot closer than a tired one.

### 6. Superpowers
43. And then there are the movie versions.
44. In the film Lucy, a drug unlocks a hundred percent of the brain, and suddenly she can read minds and move objects.
45. Real neuroscience has found no sign of any of that. Your brain can't send thoughts through the air or bend a spoon.
46. And if far too many neurons really did fire together at once? We know what that looks like. It's called a seizure. Not genius. Chaos.
47. Still, some real brains do things that look almost impossible. Kim Peek, who inspired the film Rain Man, could recall the contents of thousands of books.
48. His brain was wired very differently, and many everyday tasks were hard for him. Again and again, extreme abilities seem to come with a cost.

### The real 100%
49. So is there a real version of one hundred percent? Maybe. And you've probably felt it.
50. Time disappears. You stop worrying what others think. The task almost does itself. Psychologists call it flow. Athletes call it the zone.
51. One idea is that in flow, the parts of your brain that judge and second-guess quiet down. Not more brain. Less noise.
52. Flow shows up when a challenge is just a little harder than your skill. Too easy is boring. Too hard is stress. In between is the sweet spot.

### Showdown
53. So now you know what your brain can really do. Time to step into the ring.
54. In one corner: your brain. At its very best. In the other: the smartest machines humans have ever built.
55. Round one, speed. A computer chip ticks billions of times per second. A neuron fires a few hundred times at most. Point to the machine.
56. Round two, knowledge. Big AI models are trained on trillions of words. Reading that would take you thousands of lifetimes. Point to the machine.
57. Round three, hard maths. In twenty twenty-five, AI systems reached gold-medal scores at the International Mathematical Olympiad, the hardest maths contest for school students. Point to the machine.
58. Round four, raw power. The fastest supercomputer on Earth right now, LineShine in China, does about two quintillion calculations every second.
59. Your brain? Nobody can measure that exactly. One rough estimate from the US standards institute NIST puts it in the same league.
60. But look at the power bill. The supercomputer draws about forty-two megawatts. Your brain, twenty watts. That dim light bulb from the beginning. Roughly two million times less power. Point to you.
61. Round five, learning. Show a small child one giraffe in a picture book, and they'll often recognise the next one at the zoo. Today's AI can do that too, but only after first studying billions of images. Point to you.
62. Round six, the real world. Folding laundry, climbing stairs, catching a falling glass. Easy for you. Still surprisingly hard for robots. Point to you.
63. And feeling? An AI can write a poem about rain. But as far as anyone can tell, it has never smelled rain. That round stays open.
64. Three to three. A draw. But the most interesting result came from somewhere else: chess.
65. In two thousand five, an open tournament let anyone team up with computers. The winners weren't grandmasters. They were two amateur players with ordinary PCs, who simply worked with their machines better than anyone else.
66. Today, chess engines are so strong that this edge has mostly faded. But the idea stuck. The best results came from people and machines working together.

### Ending
67. So your brain doesn't need to run at one hundred percent. It needs the right lights on at the right time. Good sleep. Real challenges. A little less noise.
68. Let machines handle speed and storage. Keep the parts that are yours: curiosity, feeling, and the nerve to try something new.
69. Not bad for something that runs on the power of a dim light bulb. See you on the next orbit.
