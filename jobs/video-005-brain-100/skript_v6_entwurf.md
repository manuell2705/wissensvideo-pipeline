### Hook
1. Your brain is the most complex thing we know of in the universe. And most of the time, it runs in power-saving mode.
2. Eighty-six billion neurons, and only a small share of them fire at any moment. All on about twenty watts. Roughly the power of a dim light bulb.
3. So let's do something slightly irresponsible. Let's take everything science knows about the brain, and turn it all the way up.
4. Sharper senses. Perfect memory. Faster thoughts. And at the end, we'll see if this brain can keep up with the fastest computer on Earth.
5. Of course, every upgrade comes with a price. Let's find out which ones are worth paying.

### Title (Sting): "Your Brain at 100%"

### Chapter: Power-saving mode
[Ort: Gehirn-Scanner / MRT-Labor]
6. First, a quick myth to get out of the way. You've probably heard that you only use ten percent of your brain.
7. You don't. Over a single day, brain scans show practically every region getting busy. There is no secret, sleeping ninety percent.
8. But not everything runs at once. And that's not laziness. It's a budget.
9. Your brain is about two percent of your body weight, yet it burns around twenty percent of your energy. Switching everything on at once would cost more than your body could ever pay.
10. So for this video, we cancel the budget. Fantasy rules, real science. Let's start turning dials.

### Chapter: Upgrade one, the senses
[Ort: volles Café]
11. Right now, your eyes, ears and skin flood your brain with an estimated billion bits of information every second.
12. Your conscious mind works with about ten. A fire hose, feeding a drinking straw.
13. So let's remove the straw.
14. Congratulations. You now notice every sound in the room, every flicker of light, every thread of your socks. Unfortunately, you notice them all at the same time.
15. It wouldn't feel like a superpower. It would feel like living inside a fire alarm. Your filter is the reason you can hear your own name across a noisy café.
16. Some people do get a small crossover. With synaesthesia, sounds can have colours, and numbers can sit in places in space. Fascinating. But not exactly super-hearing.
17. Verdict: more input isn't the upgrade. Better filtering is. So let's leave this dial where it was, and try the next one.

### Chapter: Upgrade two, memory
[Ort: Prüfungssaal]
18. What if you never forgot anything?
19. For a few people, that's almost real. With a rare condition called hyperthymesia, they can recall nearly every day of their adult lives.
20. Pick a random Tuesday from years ago, and they'll tell you what they did. Sometimes even what the weather was like.
21. Only a few dozen cases have been confirmed by scientists. And many describe it as a mixed gift. The bad days don't fade either.
22. In the nineteen twenties, a Russian journalist named Solomon Shereshevsky could repeat long lists of numbers, even years later. But he struggled to forget. Sometimes a normal conversation was hard to follow, because every word triggered a flood of images.
23. And here's the strange part. Your memory isn't a hard drive. Every time you remember something, your brain rebuilds it, a little like retelling a story.
24a. So let's test it. A surprise quiz: forty random facts, read once. Memory dial at maximum. You ace it. Every name, every date.
24b. The next morning, you also remember every advert on the way there. Every number plate. Every awkward thing you've ever said. They all stay.
24. Forgetting isn't a bug. It's housekeeping. And it frees up room for the upgrade everyone wants: speed.

### Chapter: Upgrade three, speed
[Ort: Rennstrecke / Reaktionstest]
25. Your fastest nerve signals race along at up to one hundred and twenty metres per second. Faster than a Formula One car at full throttle.
26. And still, you need about a quarter of a second to react. See the ball. Move the hand.
26a. You can test this yourself. Someone holds a ruler, lets go without warning, and you catch it. Most people need around twenty centimetres of falling ruler before their fingers close.
27. Even "now" arrives a little late. Your brain takes a fraction of a second to assemble each moment, then quietly edits it so sound and sight feel perfectly in sync.
28. So let's push the dial. Neurons firing ten times faster.
29. Here's the catch. More firing means more energy, and more energy means more heat. Your brain works best in a narrow temperature range. A few degrees too warm, and thinking gets confused. Further, and it becomes dangerous.
30. Evolution found a smarter trick long ago. Your brain doesn't think faster. It thinks wider. Eighty-six billion neurons, around a hundred trillion connections, all working in parallel.

### Chapter: Upgrade four, the body
[Ort: Fitnessstudio]
31. Your brain isn't only a thinker. It's also a very strict bodyguard.
32. Scientists think it normally holds back how hard your muscles can pull, because full force could damage tendons and bones.
33. In extreme moments, with adrenaline flooding in, people have reported sudden bursts of strength. Maybe that's the bodyguard briefly looking away. Though honestly, most of the evidence is stories, not lab data.
33a. So, gym test. Same muscles, same bones. Only one change: the brain stops holding back.
34. So let's fire the bodyguard. Full strength. And while we're at it, no pain.
35. That sounds powerful, right up until you meet people who were born unable to feel pain. They often get badly hurt without noticing. Pain isn't the enemy. It's the smoke detector.

### Chapter: Upgrade five, learning
[Ort: Schlafzimmer bei Nacht]
36. Okay. One dial actually works. And you already own it.
37. Every time you practise something, your brain physically rewires itself.
38. London taxi drivers, who memorise thousands of streets, were found to have a larger rear part of the hippocampus, a region involved in navigation.
39. You don't need a taxi licence. In one study, people who practised juggling for three months showed measurable changes in their brain scans.
39a. Picture it at maximum: every lesson sticks the first time. A new song on the guitar, a new word, a new trick on the board.
39b. In reality, no dial goes that high. But practice, and spacing it out over days, comes surprisingly close.
40. And while you sleep, your brain replays the day and keeps what matters. A rested brain isn't at a hundred percent. But it's closer than a tired one.

### Chapter: One second at 100%
[Ort: zurück im Café]
41. But what if we really did it? Every dial, all the way up, all at once?
42. You're sitting in a café. Coffee in your hand. And now, every neuron fires at the same time.
43. The first thing you'd lose isn't your mind. It's control. Signals crash into each other. Muscles twitch. Your vision floods with light.
44. We actually know what this looks like. It's called a seizure: far too many neurons firing in sync. Not genius. Chaos.
45. So no, a hundred percent wouldn't make you Lucy from the movies. No mind-reading, no moving cups with your thoughts. Real neuroscience has found no sign of any of that.
46. Some real brains do things that look almost impossible. Kim Peek, who inspired the film Rain Man, could recall the contents of thousands of books. But his brain was wired very differently, and many everyday tasks were hard for him. Again and again, extreme abilities seem to come with a cost.

### Chapter: The fantasy round
[Weltenreise: Zeit-Tunnel → Turm mit Netz → Stadt der Gesichter → Farbwelt]
101. Okay. Time for the big fantasy questions. With a brain at one hundred percent, could you travel through time?
102. Sadly, no. Time travel isn't a brain problem. It's a physics problem. But your brain can bend how time feels.
103. In one experiment, volunteers dropped backwards from a tall tower into a safety net. Afterwards, they guessed that their own fall had lasted about a third longer than other people's falls.
104. Their brains weren't seeing in slow motion. They were saving more memories, so the moment felt longer looking back.
105. Next question. Could you read minds? No telepathy, sorry. But your brain already reads faces, voices and tiny movements, and guesses what others feel, often in a fraction of a second.
106. And could you see colours nobody else can? Here, reality gets strange. A few people may carry a fourth type of colour cell in their eyes.
107. In one study, one woman could tell apart colours that looked identical to everyone else in the test.
108. So no time machine. No mind reading. But a brain that stretches time, reads faces, and sometimes sees extra colours? Honestly, not bad.

### Chapter: The real 100%
[Ort: Skatepark / Musikprobe]
47. So is there a real version of one hundred percent? Maybe. And you've probably felt it.
48. Time disappears. You stop worrying what others think. The task almost does itself. Psychologists call it flow. Athletes call it the zone.
49. One idea is that in flow, the parts of your brain that judge and second-guess quiet down. Not more brain. Less noise.
50. It shows up when a challenge is just a little harder than your skill. Too easy is boring. Too hard is stress. In between is the sweet spot.

### Chapter: The showdown
[Ort: Arena]
51. Which brings us back to the promise from the beginning. Your brain, at its very best. Versus the smartest machines we've ever built.
52. Round one, speed. A computer chip ticks billions of times per second. A neuron fires a few hundred times at most. Point to the machine.
53. Round two, knowledge. Big AI models are trained on trillions of words. Reading that would take you thousands of lifetimes. Point to the machine.
54. Round three, hard maths. In twenty twenty-five, AI systems reached gold-medal scores at the International Mathematical Olympiad. Point to the machine.
55. Round four, raw power. The fastest supercomputer on Earth right now, LineShine in China, performs about two quintillion calculations every second.
56. Your brain? Nobody can measure that exactly. One rough estimate from the US standards institute NIST puts it in the same league.
57. But look at the power bill. The supercomputer draws about forty-two megawatts. Your brain draws twenty watts. That dim light bulb again. Roughly two million times less. Point to you.
58. Round five, learning. Show a small child one giraffe in a picture book, and they'll often recognise the next one at the zoo. Today's AI can do that too, but only after first studying billions of images. Point to you.
59. Round six, the real world. Folding laundry, climbing stairs, catching a falling glass. Easy for you. Still surprisingly hard for robots. Point to you.
60. And feeling? An AI can write a poem about rain. But as far as anyone can tell, it has never smelled rain. That round stays open.
61. Three to three. A draw. And the most interesting result came from somewhere else entirely: chess.
62. In two thousand five, an open tournament let anyone team up with computers. The winners weren't grandmasters. They were two amateur players with ordinary PCs, who simply worked with their machines better than anyone else.
63. Today, chess engines are so strong that this edge has mostly faded. But the idea stuck. Back then, people and machines working together beat both of them on their own.

### Chapter: Everything at maximum
[Ort: Control Panel, dann Montage aus allen Orten]
63a. One last experiment. Back to the control panel. This time, every dial goes all the way up. Senses, memory, speed, body, learning. And this time, we pay the bill.
63b. Learning at maximum: you could pick up a language like a small child picks up words. A new skill every week.
63c. Body at maximum: no holding back. You might lift more than ever, once. Then the tendons and bones send the invoice.
63d. Senses and memory at maximum: you'd notice everything and forget nothing. The whole world, at full volume, forever.
63e. And all of it at once? Your brain would need far more energy than your body can deliver. It's not built to run every light at once. Remember, it runs on the power of a dim bulb.
63f. So the most impressive thing about your brain isn't how much it can switch on. It's how well it knows what to switch off.

### Ending
64. So maybe power-saving mode was never the problem. Maybe it's the feature.
65. Your brain doesn't need every light on. It needs the right lights on at the right time. Good sleep. Real challenges. A little less noise.
66. Let machines handle speed and storage. Keep the parts that are yours: curiosity, feeling, and the nerve to try something new.
67. So here's a question to take with you: which dial would you turn up first?
68. Not bad for something that runs on the power of a dim light bulb. See you on the next orbit.
